Oswald Wirth Tarot — The 22 Esoteric Arcana (1889 / 1926)

Draw & reflectPlay it on recursive.ecoOpen in the card viewer

At a glance

The first tarot conceived as an explicitly occult, initiatic instrument (distinct from Etteilla's earlier divination deck of 1788) — 22 Major Arcana only, designed in 1889 by Oswald Wirth under the direction of the Kabbalist Stanislas de Guaita, realising the program of Éliphas Lévi. It fixes the marriage of tarot trumps to the Hebrew alphabet and the Kabbalah that defines Continental (and later English) esoteric tarot.

Origin & dating

Wirth, immersed in the French Occult Revival, was commissioned by de Guaita to draw 22 trumps "restored to hieroglyphic purity" per Lévi's ideas. Published 1889 as Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot Kabbalistique (hand-colored, gilt-edged like a grimoire). Wirth issued a revised, wider-known version in 1926. It reinterprets the Marseille trumps through Kabbalah, alchemy, Hermeticism and Masonic/Rosicrucian symbolism.

Provenance & evidence

The 1889 and 1926 decks and Wirth's accompanying writings survive. The lineage is explicit and documented: Lévi → de Guaita → Wirth, the French esoteric current that Lévi launched in the 1850s by asserting a tarot-Kabbalah correspondence.

Game or divination?

Esoteric/divinatory by design — never a game deck. Note it is trumps only (22 cards): a philosophical/meditative object, the Continental sibling of what the Golden Dawn was doing in England.

The fear question

Openly published, but inside a subculture. Wirth and de Guaita were public figures of a fashionable Parisian occult movement (salons, orders, presses). There was no legal peril for these bourgeois esotericists; the "secrecy" was the deliberate mystique of initiatory orders, not fear of prosecution. Contrast again the jobbing fortune-teller: respectable occult philosophy was safe; commercial fortune-telling was the criminalised, déclassé version of the same impulse.

Counter-voices

Wirth's deck embodies the esoteric tradition's own self-understanding: that it is restoring ancient symbolic truth (Lévi's Kabbalah-tarot link). The historical caveat: Lévi's Hebrew-letter assignment was an invention of the 1850s, not a medieval survival. But unlike the Egyptian myth, the Wirth deck is honest about being a construction — it openly builds a new symbolic system on the Marseille, which is exactly what the grammar should record (a deliberate esoteric overlay, well documented, not a false antiquity claim).

Open questions / corrections owed

  • Distinguish the 1889 and 1926 versions in the grammar (de Guaita-directed vs. Wirth's mature revision).
  • Wirth's Hebrew-letter/path assignments differ from the Golden Dawn's — do not conflate the two correspondence systems.

One source per deck

This deck reads Wirth's 22 plates through Wirth's own commentary and nothing else. Papus's Le Tarot des Bohémiens (1889), which used to sit on every card here, is now its own deck — `papus-tarot-des-bohemiens` — illustrated with these same plates, since Papus's book reproduced them. Same pictures, a different book about them.

Wirth's own words (1927)

Each card carries two passages from Wirth's Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge (Paris: Le Symbolisme / Émile Nourry, 1927), in French with page numbers and this site's English translation: the opening of his chapter on the card, and his Interprétations divinatoires. The French is from the BnF copy on Gallica, in the public domain (Wirth died in 1943). An earlier version of this deck carried English passages labelled as translated from this book; checked against the 1927 text, they were not Wirth's words (one called the Death card unnumbered, another made the Fool a sage), and they have been replaced. Later editions (the 1966 French reissue, the 1985 English translation The Tarot of the Magicians) are not quoted here. Wirth counts the Fool as the 22nd arcanum, with the value zero (p. 222).

Why Wirth kept divination in a book about symbols, in his words (p. 100):

Envisagée comme exercice d'assouplissement de l'esprit, la divination a l'immense mérite d'obliger le devin à penser par lui-même, en l'astreignant à deviner, donc à sonder l'obscurité, pour y distinguer ce qui s'y cache.

This site's translation: Seen as an exercise that limbers the mind, divination has the great merit of obliging the diviner to think for himself, by compelling him to guess, and so to sound the darkness and make out what is hidden there.
