Golden Dawn Tarot — Book T with Rider-Waite-Smith Imagery

Draw & reflectPlay it on recursive.ecoOpen in the card viewer

At a glance

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn is the single most important engine of modern esoteric tarot. In about twelve years (1888–1900) a small London secret society fused tarot, Kabbalah, astrology, alchemy, geomancy and Egyptian/Hermetic imagery into one graded, internally consistent system, recorded in a confidential paper called Book T. That system is the direct parent of the Rider-Waite-Smith deck and of Crowley's Thoth deck — i.e., of how almost everyone reads tarot today. It is also a textbook case of a powerful tradition built on a forged pedigree.

The founding — and the forgery

  • The Cipher Manuscripts. In 1887 William Wynn Westcott (a London coroner and Freemason) came into possession of ~60 folios in a Trithemius-style cipher, sketching Masonic-style initiation rituals built around the Kabbalistic Tree of Life and the tarot trumps.
  • The Sprengel fiction. Decoding them "revealed" the address of a German Rosicrucian adept, Anna Sprengel, who (Westcott claimed) wrote back authorising an English temple. The correspondence is almost certainly a fabrication: Decker and Dummett argue the Sprengel page was not part of the original manuscript, and that Westcott invented her to give the order an ancient continental lineage. The letters conveniently ceased in 1890.
  • Isis-Urania Temple, 1 March 1888. Westcott, Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers, and William Robert Woodman founded the order. Mathers became its creative engine, writing most of the rituals and the tarot system.

The point for the repo: the Golden Dawn's content is brilliant and historically real; its claim to ancient descent is manufactured — the esoteric equivalent of the bogus "1392" date on the Charles VI deck, but deliberate.

Book T and Mathers' tarot system

Book T: The Tarot is the order's instructional paper on the cards. It standardised the correspondences that still structure tarot meaning:

  • Each of the 22 trumps gets a Hebrew letter, a path on the Tree of Life, and an astrological attribution (a planet, element, or zodiac sign).
  • The Strength ↔ Justice swap. To keep the Hebrew-letter/zodiac sequence tidy, the GD gave Leo (Teth) to Strength and Libra (Lamed) to Justice, which seats Strength in the eighth place and Justice in the eleventh. Book T as printed in 1912 still numbers them Fortitude 11 and Justice 8; the Rider-Waite-Smith deck (1909) is the first known to print VIII Strength, XI Justice. The Marseille order has neither. Flag, don't normalise (what each source prints: `research/synthesis/strength-justice-numbering.md`).
  • The Minor Arcana get decanic meanings. Each pip (Two through Ten) is mapped to a decan (10° slice of the zodiac) ruled by a planet — e.g. the suits become Wands/Fire, Cups/Water, Swords/Air, Pentacles/Earth, and each number carries an astrological "tone." This is the grid behind modern keyword meanings (why the Ten of Swords reads as "ruin," etc.).
  • Court cards map onto the elements-within-elements and the Tree.

Crucially, Book T frames tarot as an instrument of initiation and magical philosophy, not merely fortune-telling — though divination was practised, it was the junior partner to self-transformation.

The schism (1900)

The order detonated almost as fast as it rose:

  • 1897: Westcott withdraws after his occult activities risk his public office.
  • 1900: Mathers (in Paris) tells Florence Farr that Westcott had forged the Sprengel letters — admitting the order's foundation was fraudulent. Trust collapses.
  • The Battle of Blythe Road (April 1900): Mathers sends the young Aleister Crowley — theatrically, in Highland dress and a mask — to seize the London temple's vault. W. B. Yeats and the London adepts resist; the locks are changed; lawyers and a locksmith, not magic, settle it.
  • Aftermath: the order splinters — Mathers's loyalists become Alpha et Omega (later led by his wife Moina Mathers, sister of philosopher Henri Bergson); others form Stella Matutina (where A. E. Waite and, later, Israel Regardie pass through). The systems propagate even as the original order dies.

Who was in it

A startling concentration of late-Victorian talent: poet W. B. Yeats, actress and "New Woman" Florence Farr, Aleister Crowley, A. E. Waite and Pamela Colman Smith (→ the RWS deck), Moina and MacGregor Mathers, the author Arthur Machen, and others. These were respectable professionals and artists — which is exactly why their tarot was "philosophy," not "fraud" (see the fear question).

Game or divination?

Esoteric/initiatory — never a game. Tarot here is a magical and psychological instrument; divination is included but subordinate to the Great Work of self-development.

The fear question

The Golden Dawn is the clearest illustration in the whole library that class, not the cards, decided fear. GD adepts pursued tarot in secret, oath-bound lodges — but their secrecy was initiatory mystique and professional discretion, not legal terror; a coroner and a Nobel-laureate-to-be were never going to be arrested. Meanwhile, under the Witchcraft Act 1735 (which recast "witchcraft" as fraudulent pretence) and the Vagrancy Act 1824, working-class and Romani fortune-tellers were fined and jailed for laying out the same cards for money. Same deck, same century, same city — opposite exposure to the law. Esoteric tarot was respectable; commercial cartomancy was criminal. The dividing line was who you were.

Counter-voices

The order presented its system as the recovery of a perennial Hermetic-Kabbalistic wisdom transmitted from antiquity. The scholarly read (Decker, Depaulis, Dummett) is that it is a dazzling 1880s–90s synthesis, assembled from Éliphas Lévi, Christian Kabbalah, Renaissance Hermetica, and the era's Egyptology and orientalism — a new construction in ancient dress. Two honest points pull in different directions:

  • Against the "suppressed ancient tarot" claim: the GD's antiquity is invented (the forged Sprengel lineage proves the founders themselves manufactured pedigree).
  • For the "colonial entanglement" reading: the GD's imaginative raw material — "Egyptian mysteries," "Secret Chiefs in the East," appropriated Kabbalah — is inseparable from British imperial orientalism, the Victorian habit of mining colonised cultures for exotic spiritual authority. So while the order didn't recover a suppressed Eastern tradition, it absolutely fed on the colonial image of one. That is the version of the "colonial forces" thesis that holds up: not erasure of a real tarot lineage, but extraction and re-packaging of othered cultures' prestige.

Legacy

  • Rider-Waite-Smith (1909): Waite and Smith make the GD system public through pictorial cards — see `13-rider-waite-smith.mdx`.
  • Thoth (1944): Crowley and Lady Frieda Harris push the GD framework further (referenced, never reproduced — copyright).
  • Virtually every modern "card meaning" book is downstream of Book T's correspondences.

Open questions / corrections owed

  • Distinguish in the grammar between Book T as text and the visual deck (a modern realisation of the correspondences).
  • Keep the VIII/XI swap explicit and tied to the Hebrew-letter rationale, and say where it is printed: attributions in Book T, numbers first on the 1909 cards.
  • Note the lineage forks (Alpha et Omega, Stella Matutina) so later decks can be traced to the right branch.

Lineage and where to see the decks

References only: nothing below is reproduced here, and no image from any of these decks is used in this library. "Reportedly" marks a claim that rests on one secondary source. A readable version with links: `pages/golden-dawn-decks.html`.

  • Book T (text, c. 1888–1891; printed by Crowley in The Equinox I(8), 1912). The specification every deck below works from. Public domain; its 1912 wording is on every card of this deck.
  • The Order's own pattern deck (1890s, lost). The Wang deck's published description (reproduced at learntarot.com) says it "would appear" Moina Mathers painted the original cards, and that new Adepti Minores were lent them to copy by hand. Nick Farrell (Journal of the Western Mystery Tradition no. 17, 2009) reads the record differently: the Outer Order received line drawings of some trumps and the Inner Order written descriptions, and the Order never made a deck of its own. Either way, no pattern deck is known to survive.
  • Members' hand-copied decks. The best documented is Israel Regardie's own, painted in the 1930s (reportedly). The cards were lost in the 1960s, reportedly stolen; Regardie kept photographs, which he gave to Robert Wang, and Wang reportedly sold them to a private library connected with the O.T.O. Farrell writes that Regardie's personal cards are shown in Regardie's The Complete Golden Dawn System of Magic (1984). Where to look: that book (Farrell's note cites pp. 10–12; not checked here), and a montage of the photographs on steve-p.org.
  • Whare Ra (Havelock North, New Zealand; Stella Matutina temple, 1912–1978). No coloured Whare Ra deck has been found: Farrell, who asked older members, saw only crude line drawings on small yellow cards, and writes that many members took up the Builders of the Adytum cards in the 1960s. Most regalia and records were burned when the temple closed in 1978 (Wikipedia, "Whare Ra"); by the account Nick Farrell gathered from members, one of the last chiefs, John von Dadelszen, decided it alone so that members' names would not come out (Farrell, 2019, grahamhancock.com). The story, with its sources, is the course chapter Where the cards went. Where to look: black-and-white line drawings of the Whare Ra trumps, supplied by Pat Zalewski, at the Hermetic Library's Golden Dawn Library Project. Photographs of hand-painted Whare Ra decks were not found in this search.
  • W. B. Yeats's Golden Dawn papers (c. 1890–1937), National Library of Ireland, including a notebook of Golden Dawn and tarot material. Kathleen Raine's illustrated study Yeats, the Tarot and the Golden Dawn (Dolmen Press, 1972; revised 1976) works from them. Whether they include a full painted deck was not confirmed.
  • Rider-Waite-Smith (1909). Waite and Pamela Colman Smith, both Golden Dawn members; the imagery of this deck. Public domain.
  • Thoth (painted by Lady Frieda Harris, 1938–1943; Crowley's The Book of Thoth, 1944; first printed as a deck in the late 1960s). A Golden Dawn descendant, heavily changed: renamed trumps, Crowley's own court scheme and attributions. The original paintings are at the Warburg Institute, London. In copyright.
  • The Golden Dawn Tarot (Robert Wang under Israel Regardie; 1977 per some listings, 1978 per U.S. Games listings; companion book An Introduction to the Golden Dawn Tarot, 1978). Built from Regardie's photographs and the Order papers; in Farrell's words the first complete Golden Dawn deck. Wang's own account, in his autobiography Expelled from Nursery School (2011, pp. 19–20, archived PDF): Regardie answered his letter offering to paint the Order's deck, commented on slides of each watercolour for more than a year, and later fell silent; Wang came to think the reason was his painting, and called the figures "badly drawn, strange little wooden figures". His Jungian Tarot followed, in his words an "expiation" for them (p. 24). In copyright.
  • The Hermetic Tarot (Godfrey Dowson; drawn 1975–1977, published by U.S. Games in 1980, some listings say 1979). Black and white; follows the Golden Dawn astrological attributions, loosely in places. In copyright.
  • The Ciceros' deck (Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero; The New Golden Dawn Ritual Tarot, Llewellyn, 1991; reissued as the Golden Dawn Magical Tarot, Llewellyn, 2000 or 2001, sources differ). Begun with Regardie's blessing before his death in 1985; by their own account the first deck to use the Order's flashing colours and the colour scales of the four worlds. In copyright.
  • The Zalewskis' deck (Pat and Chris Zalewski; The Magical Tarot of the Golden Dawn, first a 150-copy run in 1997 with drawings by Jonathon A. Pierce; the Aeon edition of 2008 used Richard Dudschus's redrawings of the Whare Ra cards, coloured by David Sledzinski and issued as a deck in 2022). The Whare Ra line made public. In copyright.

To compare the modern decks side by side, card by card: steve-p.org's Golden Dawn pages show the Wang, Dowson, Cicero, Wendrich/Farrell and Zalewski decks together.

Court ranks — a naming caution: Book T's courts are Knight / Queen / Prince / Princess; the RWS imagery uses King / Queen / Knight / Page. They line up by RANK as: RWS King = GD Knight, RWS Queen = GD Queen, RWS Knight = GD Prince, RWS Page = GD Princess. So where a card's ‘Golden Dawn Rank’ note equates the RWS Knight with the GD Knight, read that as a shared NAME, not a shared rank.
