Court de Gébelin's Tarot — The Birth of the 'Book of Thoth' (1781)

Draw & reflectPlay it on recursive.ecoOpen in the card viewer

At a glance

The hinge of the whole story. In 1781 the Protestant scholar Antoine Court de Gébelin published an essay declaring the Tarot de Marseille a survival of the ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth. He offered no evidence — and was wrong — but he converted a card game into a "sacred text" and launched two centuries of occult tarot. Bundled with his essay was a second piece by the Comte de Mellet, which first proposed reading the cards and linking them to the Hebrew alphabet.

Origin & dating

Published in volume 8 of de Gébelin's encyclopedic Le Monde Primitif (1781). The story runs that he saw the tarot game being played in a Paris salon (Mme Helvétius's) and "recognised" Egyptian allegory in the trumps. His redrawn plates "corrected" the Marseille images toward what he imagined Egyptian originals looked like.

Provenance & evidence

The text and plates survive and are public-domain. What does not exist is any evidentiary basis: this predates Champollion's decipherment of hieroglyphs (1822), so de Gébelin's "Egyptian" reading was pure imagination projected onto Italian Christian imagery.

Game or divination?

This is the birth of the divinatory/occult claim — but as theory, not practice. De Gébelin allegorised; it was the Comte de Mellet (and then Etteilla) who turned the idea into a method. The grammar should treat de Gébelin as the origin of the myth, explicitly dated 1781, and never as evidence about the decks' actual 15th-century origin.

The fear question

No fear at all — the opposite. De Gébelin published openly, under his name, in a respectable subscription encyclopedia read by the Parisian elite (Franklin was a subscriber). Far from hiding "occult" knowledge, he announced it as prestigious ancient wisdom. The 18th-century salon treated this as fashionable erudition, not dangerous magic. The occult overlay was, at its birth, a public, high-status intellectual fad.

Counter-voices

This file is where the "suppressed occult history" claim must be weighed most carefully. De Gébelin is the source of the Egyptian-origin belief that many practitioners still hold. Defenders argue he intuited a real esoteric substrate. The scholarly verdict (Decker, Depaulis, Dummett) is firm: the Egyptian genealogy is invented, not recovered — a Romantic-Enlightenment fantasy about Egypt, not a transmission from it. Importantly, debunking de Gébelin does not debunk the real later history of divinatory practice or its persecuted practitioners; it only removes a false pedigree.

Open questions / corrections owed

  • Clearly separate de Gébelin's essay from Mellet's appended essay — they differ (e.g. trump-to-Hebrew-letter assignments).
  • The plates are interpretive redraws; flag them as such, not as historical decks.

RESEARCH NOTE (2026-06-13). Two etymologies are often blurred: the 'Royal Road' (Tar + Ro) is Gébelin's; the 'Ta-Rosh = road/science of Thoth' is the Comte de Mellet's. Gébelin's birth year is contested across sources (1719/1724/1725/1728). The Wheel's Sphinx/Typhon/Hermanubis figures are later Lévi/Waite additions, NOT in Gébelin's 1781 plate.
