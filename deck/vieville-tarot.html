At a glance

One of the three oldest surviving named Tarots de Marseille-family decks, printed in Paris around 1650 by the maître-cartier Jacques Viéville — a near-contemporary of Jean Noblet (whose deck this library also holds). The single known copy is at the Bibliothèque nationale de France and is on Gallica (ark:/12148/btv1b10510963k). The woodcut is public domain; the BnF scan is free to reuse non-commercially with credit, and commercial reuse needs a BnF licence. Its 78 woodcut cards are shown here from the BnF scan, at high resolution.

Why it matters — the road not taken

Viéville's pack is the great outlier of the early Parisian tarots. Where Noblet sits squarely in the C-order (Marseille) line that became standard, Viéville's trump imagery leans toward the Eastern/Belgian branch: its figures face the other way, several trumps (the Popess, the Sun, the World, the Star) carry markedly different designs, and the pack is a key witness to the family that later surfaces in the Vandenborre / 'Tarot Flamand' decks rather than the Marseille mainstream. It shows that c. 1650 Paris held two competing tarot patterns at once.

What you're looking at

  • 22 atouts (trumps): 21 carry printed Roman numerals (read directly from the scans here) plus the unnumbered Mat (Fool).
  • 56 suit cards: four Italian suits — Épées (Swords), Deniers (Coins), Bâtons (Batons), Coupes (Cups) — each with King, Queen, Cavalier, Valet and ten pips.
  • The even-numbered canvases of the BnF scan are the cards' patterned backs; only the faces are shown.

Honesty notes

The trumps are identified by their printed numerals AND their scenes — and the two together reveal a key feature: the Viéville follows the older Eastern trump order, not the later Marseille standard. Here Justice is VII, the Chariot VIII, Strength VIIII, and the Hermit XI — exactly the cards the Marseille (Conver) sequence reorders. This non-standard order is itself the evidence for the deck's Eastern/Belgian-leaning lineage. Suit cards are shown in BnF folio order; courts are identified by figure, and pip ranks are assigned by position and may warrant correction — corrections welcome, as everywhere in this library.

Game, not divination

Like Noblet, this is a playing-and-trick-taking pack from a century before tarot was attached to fortune-telling; no cartomantic meaning is native to it.
