At a glance

The canonical printed standard of Western tarot — woodblock-and-stencil cards whose 22-trump sequence (C-order) became the reference layout `[Dummett1980] [Depaulis1984]`. Nicolas Conver's 1760 Marseille edition is the most renowned single witness `[Conver1760-obj] [Depaulis1984]`. Born a game deck, it is the exact pack the 18th-century occultists picked up and re-read as an Egyptian oracle `[CdG1781] [DDD1996]`.

Origin & dating

The "Marseille" pattern stabilised in 17th–18th-century French and Swiss card-making `[Dummett1980] [Depaulis1984]`. Nicolas Conver signed and printed his Marseille deck in 1760 from engraved woodblocks, coloured by stencil `[Conver1760-obj] [Depaulis1984]`. It was not the first Marseille-pattern deck — it follows earlier Marseille cardmakers such as Chosson (the well-known Chosson deck bears the date 1736, though that date is itself debated in the scholarship) `[Depaulis1984] [DDD1996]`. The Conver woodblocks were reused for over a century, and the pattern was later re-standardised for the modern era by Paul Marteau's Grimaud Ancien Tarot de Marseille (c.1930), the prototype for most 20th-century French decks `[Marteau1949] [Depaulis1984]`.

Provenance & evidence

Surviving Conver decks are held in public collections (e.g. the Bibliothèque nationale de France and the British Museum, which hold Marseille-pattern cards and sheets), and the Conver woodblocks are claimed by the successor Camoin firm `[BnF-obj] [BM-obj] [Camoin-T]`. The lineage Chosson (1736?) → Conver (1760) → Grimaud/Marteau (c.1930) is documented in the standard catalogues `[Depaulis1984] [Dummett1980]`.

Game or divination?

Game first. The Tarot de Marseille was printed and sold for the trick-taking game tarot — a living game still played in France — and the documentary record treats it as a game pack for its entire early life `[DummettMcLeod2004] [Dummett1980]`. But this is the hinge deck: because the Marseille was the common, available pattern in 1780s Paris, it is the deck Court de Gébelin allegorised as an Egyptian "Book of Thoth" in 1781, and the one Etteilla and his followers adapted into a divination system from the later 1780s `[CdG1781] [Ett1785] [DDD1996]`. So the object is a game deck and the occult tradition is a later use grafted onto it; on the documentary evidence, no divinatory reading of these trumps is attested before 1781 `[Dummett1980] [DDD1996]`. The grammar should label the deck "game," and the divinatory meanings as a documented later overlay, not an origin `[Dummett1980] [DDD1996]`.

The fear question

No fear as a game — the Tarot de Marseille was an ordinary, regulated commercial product of the French card trade, subject to the state playing-card tax and its control stamp `[Depaulis1984]`. The instructive twist comes later: by the 19th century the same physical pattern could be innocent leisure in one hand and, in another, the working tool of a prosecuted fortune-teller — prosecution that ran on vagrancy and anti-fraud law, not on anything about the cards themselves (see the fear-question discussion and statutes cited in `00-overview-history-of-tarot.mdx`) `[Dummett1980]`. The deck did not change; the use and the user's social position did.

Counter-voices

From Éliphas Lévi onward, esoteric writers treated the Marseille's "purity" as evidence of preserved ancient (Egyptian/Kabbalistic) symbolism `[Lévi1856] [DDD1996]`. The document-first reading is that the imagery is a worn, simplified descendant of the Italian trionfi, and that its apparent "perfection" is printing-tradition conservatism — block copied from block — rather than transmitted doctrine `[Dummett1980]`. That said, the Marseille's role as the canvas onto which modern esoteric tarot was built is real and historically central: it is the genuine bridge object between game and oracle `[DDD1996]`.

Open questions / corrections owed

References

[P] Primary sources (period texts and objects)

  • `[CdG1781]` Antoine Court de Gébelin, Le Monde primitif analysé et comparé avec le monde moderne, vol. VIII (Paris, 1781) — the essay declaring the tarot an Egyptian Book of Thoth; describes the very Marseille-type cards he saw in play. (public domain)
  • `[Ett1785]` Etteilla (Jean-Baptiste Alliette), Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots (Paris, 1783–1785) — the first tarot-divination method-books. (public domain)
  • `[Lévi1856]` Éliphas Lévi, Dogme et rituel de la haute magie (Paris, 1854–1856) — the 19th-century source of the tarot–Kabbalah "ancient symbolism" claim. (public domain)
  • `[Marteau1949]` Paul Marteau, Le Tarot de Marseille (Paris: Grimaud, 1949) — the standardiser's own account of the Grimaud pattern.
  • `[Conver1760-obj]` The Nicolas Conver Tarot de Marseille, Marseille, 1760 — the deck itself (signed/dated exemplars in public collections).
  • `[BnF-obj]` Bibliothèque nationale de France, Département des Estampes — Marseille-pattern tarot holdings (primary objects).
  • `[BM-obj]` British Museum — Marseille-pattern playing-card sheets and cards (primary objects; see collection online).

[S] Secondary scholarship that reads the primaries (prioritised)

  • `[Dummett1980]` Michael Dummett, The Game of Tarot: From Ferrara to Salt Lake City (London: Duckworth, 1980) — the foundational document-based history; establishes the game-first chronology and the A/B/C trump orders.
  • `[DDD1996]` Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis & Michael Dummett, A Wicked Pack of Cards: The Origins of the Occult Tarot (London: Duckworth, 1996) — traces the 1781→19th-c. occult overlay through the primary French sources (de Gébelin, Etteilla, Lévi).
  • `[Depaulis1984]` Thierry Depaulis, Tarot, jeu et magie (Paris: Bibliothèque nationale, 1984) — exhibition catalogue grounded directly in the BnF's primary holdings (Marseille cardmakers, Conver, the tax stamps).
  • `[DummettMcLeod2004]` Michael Dummett & John McLeod, A History of Games Played with the Tarot Pack (Lewiston: Edwin Mellen Press, 2004) — the definitive account of tarot as a game, including the French game still played today.

RESEARCH NOTE (2026-06-13). The elemental suit labels (Coins=Earth, Cups=Water, Swords=Air, Batons=Fire) are a later Golden Dawn overlay, not native to a 1760 game pack — read them as a modern reading aid, not the deck's own scheme. The 1760 block date sits oddly with the BnF authority record for Nicolas Conver (1784–1833); the 1760 cutting is debated. See research/decks/tarot-de-marseille-conver.md.
