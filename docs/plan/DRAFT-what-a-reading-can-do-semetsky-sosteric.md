---

Philosophers have read the same surface more ambitiously. Inna Semetsky, writing from semiotics
and the philosophy of education, treats a reading as interpretation in the sense of Peirce and
Deleuze: the card a sign, the reading a bridge between what is felt and what can be said. She
argues that tarot can serve as a counselling aid and as a way of learning from experience
([Semetsky, 2005](https://digitalcommons.trinity.edu/trickstersway/vol4/iss1/3);
[2015](https://doi.org/10.1515/sem-2015-0008)). Her frame suits this page: a card is a sign to
interpret, not an instruction. Where she goes further, writing that the cards laid in a spread
are chosen by the unconscious and "cannot be considered random" (as quoted by Sosteric, 2014),
the evidence in [Why a Reading Feels So Personal](course-viewer.html?course=why-a-reading-feels-personal)
does not follow her, and neither does this page. The sharpest critic of this whole archetypal
vocabulary comes from sociology. Mike Sosteric argues that the occult tarot's meanings, and the
Jungian language later wrapped around them, carry the ideology of the elites who wrote them
(*Canadian Journal of Sociology*, 2014). You don't have to accept his history to take his warning:
of any meaning a card seems to carry, ask who wrote it into the card, and whether you would
choose it yourself.
