# Tarot as a Source of Fiction

I want to look at a narrower question than the one this site usually asks. Not *does a reading help the person holding the cards* — [How the Cards Can Work](course-viewer.html?course=how-tarot-works) already makes that case — but a stranger one: **can a deck of cards write your book for you?** One writer actually tried this, in the open, and told us exactly what happened when he did.

## The machine for constructing stories

In 1973 Italo Calvino published *The Castle of Crossed Destinies*, a novel in which travelers who have lost the power of speech tell their life stories entirely by laying tarot cards on a table. He didn't just write *about* the method. He used it. In his own afterword he says he laid grids of cards — the Visconti-Sforza deck, in the 1969 Ricci facsimile, for the first half of the book, the Marseille pattern for the second — and read narratives out of the rows, columns, and diagonals, the way you'd read words out of a crossword. His own name for what he'd built: **"a machine for constructing stories."**

That is a genuinely different claim from the one this site usually makes about a reading. It isn't "the image helps me think." It's "the arrangement of cards, followed as a rule, produces a story I would not otherwise have written." Cards first, story assembled after — the opposite direction from an author who already knows the ending and goes looking for cards to dress it in.

**A small demonstration, with real cards.** Not a full grid — just one row, from this library's own facsimile of the deck Calvino names for the book's first half.

Read left to right the way Calvino read a row, and a shape turns up that nobody decided in advance: someone setting out holding nothing (the Fool), a reversal that isn't chosen (the Wheel), an ending that is also a passage (Death). That isn't a fortune for you — it's the mechanism, laid bare, three cards from this library's own [Visconti-Sforza deck](../viewers/cards.html?src=../tarot/visconti-sforza-tarot/grammar.json), the same facsimile edition Calvino names in his afterword; the [Marseille pattern](../viewers/cards.html?src=../tarot/tarot-de-marseille-conver/grammar.json) is what he switched to for the book's second half. If you want to lay out a real grid of your own — rows, columns, and diagonals to read across, the actual method rather than a demonstration of it — this library's [Caster](../viewers/caster.html) will do it.

## The confession, which is the best part

Here is what makes Calvino worth an entire course rather than a footnote: he didn't let himself off easy, and he told the reader so. In that same afterword he admits to constant backsliding — nights spent tearing up a "perfect" arrangement he'd found the morning after, tales he already wanted to tell that he then forced into the grid regardless of whether they belonged there. When that happened, he says plainly, the whole exercise became **gratuitous**. He is not a clean demonstration of a fiction machine. He is a clean demonstration of *disclosing* exactly where the machine failed him.

That distinction — announcing the seam instead of hiding it — is the same discipline this project asks of its own history pages: read [A History of Tarot](course-viewer.html?course=history-of-tarot) and you'll find dates, names, and the plain admission that the divinatory meanings arrived three and a half centuries after the cards themselves, attached by people who can be named. Calvino is doing the literary version of the same thing. Own the invention. Say where you made it up. That is not a weaker kind of confidence than asserting your invention as fact — it is a different, more honest kind.

## The Oulipo cousin: rule, not belief

Calvino wasn't working alone in spirit. He belonged to the loose circle around Raymond Queneau's Oulipo, writers who built novels under constraints chosen on purpose — a book with no letter *e*, a character's movements dictated by chess moves. Calvino's own defense of the method, elsewhere in his essays, is that **the constraint's elegance is what matters, independent of any claim that it accesses something beyond itself.** He is not saying the tarot channels anything. He is saying: here is a rule, and a rule followed honestly can generate something a free hand never would have.

That is this site's whole argument about a reading — [Intention Setting](course-viewer.html?course=intention-setting)'s *"relate to the card; never obey it"* — arrived at from the opposite end. A reader asks a card to be useful without asking it to be true. Calvino asked the same of a grid of cards, and got a novel out of it instead of an afternoon's insight.

## The company it keeps — and the one contrast that matters

Calvino isn't the only writer who reached for a divination system as a tool rather than a topic, and the differences between them are the interesting part.

**Philip K. Dick and Ursula K. Le Guin** both used the I Ching's yarrow-stalk casting as an actual working method — Dick most visibly, letting cast hexagrams decide plot turns in *The Man in the High Castle*. That's a different move from Calvino's: not a rule chosen for its elegance, but chance treated as a channel to something the writer didn't consciously hold. It belongs with the older traditions this site already surveys in [How Humans Have Cast Lots](course-viewer.html?course=divination-traditions) — read that course for the fuller company the I Ching keeps, going back to oracle bones and yarrow stalks. I mention Dick and Le Guin here only to mark the fork in the road: two writers, two ways of using structured randomness to write, and neither one is obviously the "real" way.

**T. S. Eliot** is the useful negative case. Madame Sosostris deals tarot cards in *The Waste Land* — cards that don't correspond to any real deck, which Eliot cheerfully admitted in his own endnotes. The tarot is *content* in that poem, texture borrowed for its prophetic weight. There's no evidence it *generated* anything. Worth holding onto: a famous work featuring tarot is not the same claim as a famous work built by tarot, and the gap between those two claims is exactly what this whole course has been trying to keep sharp.

**W. B. Yeats** isn't a tarot case at all, but he's close enough kin to be worth one paragraph. His 1925 book *A Vision* builds an entire symbolic system — twenty-eight phases of the moon, standing in relation to human character much the way this site's Major Arcana stand in relation to a life — out of automatic-writing sessions with his wife, George. Yeats wrapped that real oracular material in a fictional cover story, characters recovering ancient wisdom from a nomadic tribe, to hide where the system had actually come from. He eventually admitted the device, years later. It's the mirror image of Calvino's choice: Calvino disclosed his seams as he made them; Yeats concealed his, and only unconcealed them afterward. Both wrote something real. Only one of them let the reader watch it happen.

## What a fiction machine has to risk

There's a sharper way to say what separates a real "machine for constructing stories" from a card trick, and it comes from outside tarot entirely: the critic Michael Fried's idea of **objecthood** — a made thing judged only on whether the result is interesting, with nothing actually at stake in how it turned out. On that reading, a shuffle-and-see exercise is a curiosity exactly once. What saves Calvino's grid from that fate is that it still has to resolve into a novel — an inherited form the cards are not allowed to shrug off.

This site has its own version of that same requirement, worn into the language of every course here: a card asked with a real question behind it is a gate; a card pulled with nothing riding on the answer is closer to a toy. The two failures aren't the same failure — mistaking a made system for a found truth is one kind of mistake, and treating your own made system as though nothing depends on it is the opposite kind — but a reading and a novel-written-from-a-grid both need to dodge both of them to be worth the time they take.

There's a worked example of exactly this risk one page over: **[The Book of Fate](book-of-fate-1822.html)**, a public-domain 1822 oracle chapbook whose lookup mechanism is startlingly close to Calvino's own — a cast Sign points to one fixed Hieroglyphic, and the Hieroglyphic points to one fixed answer. Most of its questions, as far as this library has verified so far, turn out to have exactly one recorded answer no matter how you cast — which could read as objecthood at its flattest, a wheel that always lands on the same slot. What saves it from that reading is the same thing that saves Calvino: the page says so, out loud, instead of letting a cast feel more alive than the source actually is.
