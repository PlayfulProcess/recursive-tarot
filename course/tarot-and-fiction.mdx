# Tarot as a Source of Fiction

I want to ask a narrower question than the one this site usually asks. Not *does a reading help the person holding the cards* — [How the Cards Can Work](course-viewer.html?course=how-tarot-works) already makes that case, carefully — but a stranger one:

**Can a deck of cards write your book for you?**

Not inspire it. Not decorate it. Write it — supply the plot, the turns, the order of events, so that the author becomes something closer to a reader of their own layout. One major novelist tried exactly this, on purpose, published the result, and then wrote a closing note explaining precisely how it had gone. That note is the most useful document I know of on this whole question, because it is the rare case of a writer showing the machine *and* admitting where he stopped using it.

This course is about him: who he was, how he ended up with the cards in the first place, what the method actually was, what it produced, and what it cost him. Then the company he keeps, and the thing any fiction machine has to risk to be worth building.

## 1. The man, and why the answer matters

**Italo Calvino** was born in 1923 in Santiago de las Vegas, Cuba, where his parents — both scientists, his father an agronomist, his mother a botanist — were working at an agricultural station. The family returned to San Remo, on the Ligurian coast, when he was small. At twenty he went into the mountains with the Garibaldi partisan brigades and fought the last two years of the war there; his first novel, *The Path to the Nest of Spiders* (1947), came straight out of it.

What he did for most of his working life was not "be a novelist" in the garret sense. He was an **editor at Einaudi in Turin** for decades — a publishing professional, reading other people's manuscripts, sitting in meetings. Alongside that he wrote the books he is loved for: the fabulist *Our Ancestors* trilogy in the fifties, then, after moving to Paris in 1967, the run that made him internationally famous — *Invisible Cities* (1972), *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1973), *If on a winter's night a traveller* (1979). He died in Siena in 1985, of a stroke, partway through writing the Harvard lectures published afterwards as *Six Memos for the Next Millennium*.

Here is the part that matters for us, and it is worth being blunt about it: **nothing in that life is occult.** Calvino was not a seeker. He was not looking for hidden knowledge in the cards and he never claimed the tarot knew anything. In Paris he fell in with the **Oulipo**, the workshop around Raymond Queneau devoted to writing under invented constraints — a novel without the letter *e*, a text whose movements are dictated by a knight's tour on a chessboard. In a lecture he gave in 1967, later collected in English as *The Literature Machine*, he had already described the writing of stories as essentially combinatorial: a finite set of elements and functions, shuffled and recombined.

A writer who thinks that way does not need a deck of cards to be magic. He needs it to be **a machine** — and that is exactly what he called it.

## 2. A commission, not a calling

The origin of the book is less mystical than almost anyone expects, and I think it is the single most clarifying fact in this course.

In 1969 the Parma publisher **Franco Maria Ricci** — famous for extravagantly produced art books — was bringing out the first complete photographic reproduction of a fifteenth-century tarot deck, the Visconti pack split today between the Accademia Carrara in Bergamo and the Morgan Library in New York, painted around the middle of the 1400s and generally attributed to **Bonifacio Bembo**. You can look at that deck in this library right now: [the Visconti-Sforza cards](../viewers/cards.html?src=../tarot/visconti-sforza-tarot/grammar.json), and [Bembo's own entry](../viewers/cards.html?src=../tarot/people-of-tarot/grammar.json) among the people behind the decks.

Ricci asked Calvino for an accompanying text — the kind of literary essay a luxury facsimile carries at the front. Calvino did not write an essay. He wrote a set of interlocking stories generated *from the cards being reproduced*, and it went into the 1969 volume as *Tarocchi: il mazzo visconteo di Bergamo e New York*.

Four years later he built a second half by the same method, this time using the [Marseille pattern](../viewers/cards.html?src=../tarot/tarot-de-marseille-conver/grammar.json) — a cheaper, later, mass-printed deck, and a deliberate contrast to the gold-leaf Visconti. Einaudi published the pair in 1973 as *Il castello dei destini incrociati*: **The Castle of Crossed Destinies**, in which travellers who have lost the power of speech tell their life stories by laying tarot cards on a table, and **The Tavern of Crossed Destinies**, doing it again with rougher cards and rougher lives.

So his interest in tarot began as a job. He was handed seventy-eight pictures, asked for some words, and decided to see whether the pictures could produce the words. That is a different origin from every other tarot story on this site, and it is the reason his testimony is so useful: he had no stake whatsoever in the cards being profound.

## 3. The machine

Here is his own description of what he found, and it is the sentence this entire course hangs on:

> "I realized the tarots were a machine for constructing stories."
>
> — **Italo Calvino**, from the closing note to *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1973; translated by William Weaver)

Not a mirror. Not a channel. A **machine** — something with inputs, a rule, and an output.

The rule worked like this. He laid the cards out in a grid on the table, face up. Then he read the grid the way you read a crossword: across the rows, down the columns, and diagonally — so that any single card sat inside two or three different narratives at once, meaning one thing to the story running left-to-right and something else to the story running top-to-bottom. Each traveller's tale had to be told with the cards they had, in the order the layout gave them, and had to survive being crossed by every neighbouring tale.

That constraint is much tighter than it sounds. A card that works beautifully as "a betrayal" in one row may have to double as "an inheritance" in the column that crosses it. Move one card and two stories break. It is closer to building a crossword than to writing a chapter — which is precisely why an Oulipo writer would find it interesting, and why nobody needed to believe in anything for it to work.

**A demonstration, with real cards.** Not a full grid — just one row, from this library's own facsimile of the very deck Calvino was given.

Now imagine you are *also* required to make the middle card mean something entirely different to a story running vertically through it. That is the job.

## 4. What it produced — a worked example

The best surviving demonstration of the method in action is the passage where Calvino sends **Astolpho to the moon** — a borrowing from Ariosto's *Orlando Furioso*, the epic in which the knight Astolpho flies to the moon to recover Orlando's lost wits. Calvino runs the episode off two cards, and you can watch the pictures turn into prose:

The **Judgement** card — angels above, bodies rising — supplies the ascent: the trumpet and the upward motion become Astolpho's flight to the pale fields of the moon. Then a **Cups** card, with its vessels ranged in a row, supplies what he finds when he gets there: a storehouse of phials, lined up like the cups on the card, each holding something a human being never got to live — the thoughts that knocked once at the threshold of awareness and vanished, the possibilities discarded in the game of combinations, the solutions that could have been reached and never were.

Look at what has happened there. The image on the card did not *symbolise* the lost lives; the row of cups **is** the row of phials. The picture is not standing in for the idea, it is generating it. And the idea it generates is, of all things, a description of a combinatorial machine: a warehouse of the outcomes a shuffle didn't produce. The method wrote a passage about the method.

That is the machine working at full power. It is also, as he then admits, not how the whole book got written.

## 5. The confession, which is the best part

Here is why Calvino earns a course rather than a footnote. In his closing note he does not sell the method. He audits it.

He describes getting stuck for long stretches; deciding the whole operation was absurd and abandoning it; waking in the night with a correction that then unravelled every other placement; going to bed satisfied that he had finally found the pattern and destroying that solution in the morning. And — the important one — he admits that he sometimes gave up on the grid entirely and simply wrote the stories that had already taken shape in his head, without worrying whether they fitted the network of the others. When he did that, he says plainly, the exercise became **gratuitous**: the cards were no longer generating anything, they were decorating a decision he had already made.

That is not a clean demonstration of a fiction machine. It is something better: a clean demonstration of **disclosing** where the machine failed. He tells you which parts the cards wrote and which parts he wrote and then blamed on the cards.

Announcing the seam instead of hiding it is exactly the discipline this project asks of its own history pages. Read [A History of Tarot](course-viewer.html?course=history-of-tarot) and you will find dates, names, and the plain admission that the divinatory meanings arrived three and a half centuries after the cards, attached by people who can be named and dated. Calvino is doing the literary version of the same move. Own the invention; say where you made it up. That is not a weaker kind of confidence than presenting your invention as fact. It is a harder one.

## 6. The third part he never wrote

He planned a third panel, and the reason it failed is the most interesting unfinished business in this whole subject.

It was to be called **The Motel of Crossed Destinies**, and the deck would not have been tarot at all — it would have been **comic strips**. The frame: survivors of some mysterious catastrophe take shelter in a half-destroyed motel, unable to speak, and the only printed matter left intact is a scorched newspaper comics page. They tell their stories by pointing at its panels — gangsters, terrified women, spacecraft, vamps, aerial warfare, mad scientists.

He abandoned it. The reason he gives is that he could not settle what the tarot's *contemporary* equivalent actually is — what set of pictures does for us now what seventy-eight painted cards did for the fifteenth century, functioning as a shared stock of images a whole culture can read without instruction.

It is a live question, and it is genuinely open. Advertising did that job for a while. Film stills. Emoji, arguably, in a thin way. Whatever the answer is, note the shape of the requirement: a fiction machine needs a **finite, fixed, publicly legible set of images** — small enough to learn, rich enough to recombine, and already meaningful to the reader before the writer touches them. That is the specification. The tarot happens to meet it. Not much else does.

## 7. The company it keeps — and the contrast that matters

Calvino is not the only writer who reached for a divination system as a tool rather than a topic, and the differences between them are the interesting part.

**Philip K. Dick and Ursula K. Le Guin** both used the I Ching's yarrow-stalk casting as a genuine working method — Dick most visibly, letting cast hexagrams decide plot turns in *The Man in the High Castle*. That is a different move from Calvino's: not a rule chosen for its elegance, but chance treated as access to something the writer did not consciously hold. It belongs with the older traditions this site surveys in [How Humans Have Cast Lots](course-viewer.html?course=divination-traditions) — read that for the fuller company the I Ching keeps, back to yarrow stalks and oracle bones. I flag them here only to mark the fork: two ways of using structured randomness to write, and neither is obviously the "real" one.

**T. S. Eliot** is the useful negative case. Madame Sosostris deals tarot cards in *The Waste Land* — cards that correspond to no actual deck, as Eliot cheerfully admitted in his own endnotes. The tarot there is *content*: texture borrowed for its prophetic weight. There is no evidence it generated anything. Worth keeping sharp: a famous work **featuring** tarot is not the same claim as a famous work **built by** tarot, and most writing on this subject slides between the two without noticing.

**W. B. Yeats** is not a tarot case at all, but he is close kin. His 1925 book *A Vision* builds a full symbolic system — twenty-eight phases of the moon, standing to human character roughly as the Major Arcana stand to a life — out of automatic-writing sessions with his wife, George. Yeats then wrapped that real material in a fictional cover story about characters recovering ancient wisdom from a nomadic tribe, to conceal where the system had actually come from; he admitted the device only years later. It is the mirror image of Calvino's choice. Calvino disclosed his seams while making them; Yeats hid his and unpicked the disguise afterwards. Both produced something real. Only one let the reader watch it happen.

## 8. What a fiction machine has to risk

There is a sharper way to say what separates a real machine for constructing stories from a card trick, and it comes from outside tarot entirely: the critic Michael Fried's idea of **objecthood** — a made thing judged only on whether the result came out interesting, with nothing actually at stake in how it turned out. On that reading, shuffle-and-see is a curiosity exactly once. What saves Calvino's grid is that it still has to resolve into a novel — an inherited form the cards are not allowed to shrug off. The constraint has something to answer to.

This site has its own version of that requirement, worn into the language of every course here: a card asked with a real question behind it is a gate; a card pulled with nothing riding on the answer is closer to a toy. The two failures are not the same failure — mistaking a made system for a found truth is one kind of error, and treating your own made system as though nothing depends on it is the opposite kind — but a reading and a novel-written-from-a-grid have to dodge both to be worth the time.

There is a worked example of this risk one page over: **[The Book of Fate](book-of-fate-1822.html)**, a public-domain 1822 oracle chapbook whose mechanism is startlingly close to Calvino's — a cast Sign points to a fixed Hieroglyphic, and the Hieroglyphic points to a fixed answer. It is a real machine, and it was also a commercial fraud that invented an Egyptian pedigree to sell itself. Both things at once, which is the useful part.

## 9. Try the method yourself

You do not need permission or a belief system to use this. You need a deck, a grid, and a rule you agree to keep.

1. **Lay a grid, not a line.** Three by three is plenty. Use the [Caster](../viewers/caster.html) in this library, or physical cards.
2. **Read every row and every column.** Each card has to carry a role in the horizontal story *and* a different role in the vertical one. That double duty is the whole engine — it is what stops you writing what you were going to write anyway.
3. **Do not move a card because you dislike what it gives you.** The moment you rearrange to suit a story you already had, you are Calvino on a bad night, and the exercise has gone gratuitous.
4. **Write down where you cheated.** You will cheat. The note at the end is the part that keeps the method honest — and, per this course's whole argument, the part that turns a shuffle into a piece of work.
