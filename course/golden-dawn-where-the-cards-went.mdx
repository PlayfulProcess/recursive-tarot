## A deck that was never printed

The Golden Dawn wrote its tarot down. *Book T* gives every card a title, an attribution and a description, and it was [printed in *The Equinox* in 1912](https://archive.org/details/IAPSOP-equinox_v1_n8_1912_Sep). What the Order did not do was print cards.

Nick Farrell, who in Hawke's Bay worked with former members of the New Zealand temple, puts it plainly: the Golden Dawn never built its own deck. In his account, the Outer Order showed members line drawings of some of the trumps, left uncoloured because the colours were Inner Order teaching. The Inner Order received written details of the court cards and the small cards. Until the Rider-Waite-Smith deck of 1909, an adept who needed cards for Inner Order work had to use a traditional deck ([Farrell, *Journal of the Western Mystery Tradition* no. 17, 2009](http://www.jwmt.org/v2n17/farrell.html)).

The other account is about copying. The published description of the 1978 Wang deck says that Moina Mathers appears to have painted the original cards, that they were never printed, and that each new Adeptus Minor was lent the deck and hand-painted a copy ([reproduced at learntarot.com](https://learntarot.com/gddesc.htm)). Steve P., who has compared the modern decks card by card, writes that the Order probably had one or two specimen decks, since lost, and that it is not known for certain whether members copied a specimen, worked from the descriptions, or both ([steve-p.org](https://www.steve-p.org/cards/GDaw.html)). This library's own dossier follows the copying account ([the Golden Dawn dossier](https://github.com/PlayfulProcess/recursive-tarot/blob/main/research/people/golden-dawn.md)).

Copying by hand was how everything moved in the Order. Teaching papers were lent, copied out, and sent back. A label on the folder members kept them in, as reproduced in a modern edition of the Order's *Flying Rolls*, ends with an instruction to the member's executors: in case of death, return the papers to the Order "at once, unread and unopened" ([Thelemagick Library edition](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/Flying_Rolls.html)). Hold on to that label. It explains a fire later in this chapter.

## Moina's cards

Moina Mathers was born Mina Bergson in Geneva in 1865, the sister of the philosopher Henri Bergson. She trained at the Slade School of Art in London, was the first person initiated into the Golden Dawn, in March 1888, and married MacGregor Mathers in 1890. She designed regalia and temple furnishings and, Wikipedia says, some of the original designs for the Golden Dawn tarot ([Wikipedia, "Moina Mathers"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moina_Mathers)).

Her pattern deck, if it was hers and if it was a full deck, is lost. The nearest thing in print is a set of line drawings of the sixteen court cards found among the papers of William Wynn Westcott, one of the three founders, and published in 1996 as *The Golden Dawn Court Cards* (Golden Dawn Studies no. 5, edited by Darcy Küntz). Listings credit the drawings to Westcott, to Moina Mathers, or to both, and the attribution is unsettled ([the Golden Dawn Research Trust listing](https://www.goldendawntrust.com/v05-golden-dawn-court-cards)).

Farrell's account fits the Westcott side. Robert Felkin, chief of the Stella Matutina, one of the orders the Golden Dawn split into after 1900, asked Westcott to flesh out a fuller pack; Westcott worked on the court cards and gave Felkin an outline for the rest. Felkin took the result into his own order, and above all to New Zealand ([Farrell 2009](http://www.jwmt.org/v2n17/farrell.html)).

## The house of the sun

Havelock North is a small town in Hawke's Bay, on New Zealand's North Island. In 1912 Felkin arrived there, initiated a group of local seekers, and warranted a temple. Its home was a house built for it, named Whare Ra, "house of the sun", with the temple in the basement. The architect, James Chapman-Taylor, was a senior member, and he built in reinforced concrete at a time when builders there still resisted anything but timber. It was the only Golden Dawn temple to work in a permanent building made for it ([Wikipedia, "Whare Ra"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whare_Ra); [Heritage New Zealand listing](https://www.heritage.org.nz/list-details/4407/Whare-Ra)).

Felkin moved there for good in 1916 and died in 1926. A candidate for initiation reportedly entered the temple by a staircase hidden behind a wardrobe in Felkin's surgery. The 1931 Hawke's Bay earthquake levelled or damaged many buildings; at Whare Ra, the one recorded damage was the temple's Black Pillar toppling onto the paw of a sphinx. At its height in the 1930s the temple is estimated to have had about three hundred members, reportedly including two Anglican bishops and a governor-general (all from *The Lantern*, an anonymous history of the temple published in three volumes from 2012, as cited by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whare_Ra)). Farrell adds that the New Zealand papers never reported on Whare Ra; one of its founders owned the local paper ([Farrell 2019](https://grahamhancock.com/farrelln1/)).

So what did their cards look like? Farrell asked older members and found no coloured deck among them. The only versions he saw were small pieces of yellow card with simple, crude line drawings. In the 1960s, he writes, many members joined the Builders of the Adytum and replaced their Golden Dawn sets with Paul Foster Case's cards ([Farrell 2009](http://www.jwmt.org/v2n17/farrell.html)).

There is a small loop in that switch. Case had left the American branch of Moina Mathers' order after a disagreement with her and founded the Builders of the Adytum in 1922. His trumps were sold in black and white, because he held that every student should colour their own ([Wikipedia, "Builders of the Adytum"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Builders_of_the_Adytum); ["B.O.T.A. tarot deck"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.O.T.A._tarot_deck)). The Whare Ra members who traded their cards in were still colouring cards by hand.

A second witness agrees with Farrell about the drawing. Pat Zalewski, who worked with Whare Ra material from the 1980s, says he redrew the temple's cards with the artist Skip Dudschus because the originals were "badly drawn to the point of being ridiculous" (quoted at [steve-p.org](https://www.steve-p.org/cards/Zale.html)). So Whare Ra decks existed. No photograph of a hand-painted one was found for this course.

## Why the papers were burned

In August 1978 a letter went to every member: the temple was closing, and there would be no Vernal Equinox ceremony. The letter gave its reasons. Attendance was thin, and so, it said, was the power in the temple; no new member had been admitted since 1975, and there had been no grade ceremonies for two years or more (the letter, as printed in *The Lantern* vol. III, 2019, and quoted by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whare_Ra); some web pages date it the 28th rather than the 24th).

The letter explains the closing. The fire has its own account, and it comes from Farrell, who spoke to members while writing *Beyond the Sun*, his book on the temple's history and inner teachings. In a 2019 article he says the decision fell to John von Dadelszen, one of the last three chiefs, who by the mid-1970s felt he had lost the inner contact the Order's leaders had long worked by. Von Dadelszen, Farrell writes, decided without consulting the others to close Whare Ra, sell the house, and put the money into a New Age project at Taupō called Tauhara. Members were allowed to keep their own papers. Sixty years of the Order's papers went onto a bonfire that reportedly burned for a whole weekend. His reason, in Farrell's telling, was that he did not want the members' names from the strange temple on the hill to come out ([Farrell, "Beyond the Sun: The Occult Order That Ran a Town", 2019](https://grahamhancock.com/farrelln1/)).

Von Dadelszen was a public man, and Farrell identifies him as the town's mayor. The New Zealand Law Society's obituary of John Herman von Dadelszen (1913–1988) describes a lawyer of more than fifty years' practice who was a councillor and mayor of Havelock North and a Hastings coroner, and does not mention Whare Ra ([obituary](https://www.lawsociety.org.nz/news/newsroom/obituaries/john-herman-von-dadelszen-1913-1988/)). Farrell says that as a local reporter he later wrote von Dadelszen's obituary himself, and that none of the members he spoke to for it mentioned the temple. (He places that in the 1990s; the Law Society gives the death as 1988.)

Two points where the sources do not line up. Wikipedia says the temple's people burned most of the regalia, furnishings and records, as if it were the chiefs together; Farrell says one man decided alone. And Farrell's account of the fire rests on what members told him, so it is second-hand: it is the most detailed account found, not a document.

The fire also had a history behind it. A burned or buried temple was an old ending in this tradition:

- **The rule of trust.** Papers were lent, never owned, and were to go back "unread and unopened" when a member died. The modern editor Frater Ad Alta, introducing the *Flying Rolls*, writes that members destroyed a great many papers to keep the Order secret, usually by fire, and names the 1978 Whare Ra fire as the latest case, one he regrets ([Thelemagick Library](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/Flying_Rolls.html)).
- **Alpha et Omega, 1939.** Moina Mathers' order closed at the outbreak of the Second World War. The painter Ithell Colquhoun, a cousin of Edward Langford-Garstin, one of its last London leaders, wrote in *Sword of Wisdom* (1975) that its temple furniture was destroyed on the instruction of the Order's Secret Chiefs and burned at Sacombe Park in Hertfordshire. In 1966 a box of the same order's ritual tools turned up on a beach after a cliff gave way, and a newspaper photograph said they had belonged to a witch (both via [Wikipedia, "Alpha et Omega"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_et_Omega), citing Colquhoun 1975 and Francis King 1971).
- **Names were the risk.** Westcott, a London coroner, as von Dadelszen was a Hastings one, left the Golden Dawn in 1896 or 1897. Historians have speculated that Order papers he left in a hansom cab were found and brought to his employers' attention ([Wikipedia, "Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermetic_Order_of_the_Golden_Dawn), citing King 1989).

What did the fire take? Not the system. Israel Regardie had printed the Stella Matutina's rituals and teaching papers in four volumes in 1938–40, breaking his oath of secrecy ([Wikipedia, "Israel Regardie"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Regardie)). What burned was the temple's own record: who joined, when, and what they did in that basement for sixty years. The pillars and the two sphinxes survived, and members' copies of rituals and lectures were passed on ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whare_Ra)); Farrell's *Beyond the Sun* prints the temple's Second Order rituals and training papers, and more are at the [Hermetic Library's Whare Ra papers](https://hermetic.com/gdlibrary/whare_ra/index). Whether any tarot cards went into the fire, no source found here says.

**ed.** The fire kept the Order's promise to its members and lost a history to everyone else. Both are true at once, and the sources leave them side by side.

## Regardie's deck, in photographs

The member's deck that can still be seen belonged to the man who broke the silence. Regardie joined the Stella Matutina in 1934, learned the cards, Farrell says, at the order's Bristol temple from the same Felkin set Whare Ra used, grew disillusioned with its leaders, and published ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Regardie); [Farrell 2009](http://www.jwmt.org/v2n17/farrell.html)).

He also painted his own cards, reportedly in the 1930s; Robert Wang later described them as hastily made. The cards vanished in the 1960s, believed stolen. Regardie had kept a full set of photographs. He gave them to Wang as the guide for a new deck, and Wang reportedly sold them to a private library connected with the O.T.O., where they are hard to reach ([steve-p.org](https://www.steve-p.org/cards/GDaw.html)).

Where to see them: a montage of the photographs on [steve-p.org's Wang page](https://www.steve-p.org/cards/GDaw.html), with Regardie's Temperance beside Wang's. Farrell writes that Regardie's personal cards are shown in Regardie's *The Complete Golden Dawn System of Magic* (1984); his note cites pages 10–12, which were not checked for this course.

So the hand-painted Golden Dawn deck you can look at today is a set of photographs of a deck that no longer exists.

## The descendants

Every deck below works from Book T, more or less closely. In copyright, all but the first: named and linked, not shown.

**Rider-Waite-Smith, 1909.** A. E. Waite's scheme, drawn by Pamela Colman Smith, both members of the Order. It follows the Golden Dawn order of Strength (VIII) and Justice (XI) and, by design, veils some of the Order's attributions. The pictures this library uses: [the deck in the library](../viewers/cards.html?src=../tarot/rider-waite-smith-pictorial-key/grammar.json), and [the course's chapter on it](course-viewer.html?course=walking-the-golden-dawn-path).

**Thoth, painted 1938–1943.** Aleister Crowley, initiated into the Golden Dawn in 1898, and the painter Lady Frieda Harris. A descendant that argues with its parent: renamed trumps and some attributions moved. Crowley's *The Book of Thoth* came out in 1944; the cards were first printed in 1968, in monochrome, and in colour from 1972. Harris's paintings are kept at the Warburg Institute in London ([Wikipedia, "Thoth Tarot"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth_Tarot)).

**The Golden Dawn Tarot, 1977 or 1978.** Painted by Robert Wang under Regardie's direction, from the photographs and the Order's papers; Farrell calls it the first complete Golden Dawn deck. Wang said his cards were not strict copies of Regardie's, and he blamed the Felkins for altering the Order's documents and tarot. Years later he wrote that the real reason for a rift with Regardie was his own painting, which he had come to dislike ([steve-p.org](https://www.steve-p.org/cards/GDaw.html); [publisher](https://www.usgamesinc.com/the-golden-dawn-tarot.html)).

**The Hermetic Tarot, 1979 or 1980.** Godfrey Dowson's black-and-white drawings, made between 1975 and 1977 and published by U.S. Games. In steve-p.org's comparison it follows Book T more loosely than the other decks here ([steve-p.org](https://steve-p.org/cards/Herm.html)).

**The Ciceros' deck, 1991.** Chic and Sandra Tabatha Cicero, *The New Golden Dawn Ritual Tarot* (Llewellyn), begun with Regardie's blessing; he died in 1985, while it was being designed. By the makers' account it was the first tarot to use the Order's flashing colours and the colour scales of the four worlds. Later reissued as the *Golden Dawn Magical Tarot* ([steve-p.org](https://steve-p.org/cards/Cice.html)).

**The Zalewskis' deck: Whare Ra in print.** Pat and Chris Zalewski's *The Magical Tarot of the Golden Dawn* began as a 150-copy book in 1997, illustrated by Jonathon A. Pierce. The 2008 edition replaced those drawings with Dudschus's redrawings of the Whare Ra cards, and in 2022 David Sledzinski's colouring of them was issued as a deck ([steve-p.org](https://steve-p.org/cards/Zale.html)). Black-and-white line drawings of the Whare Ra trumps, supplied by Zalewski, are at the [Hermetic Library](https://hermetic.com/gdlibrary/tarot/whare_ra/index). From small yellow cards to a boxed deck took more than forty years after the fire.

Farrell's 2009 article was itself written about a further deck, made with Harry and Nicola Wendrich for his own temple; steve-p.org shows it beside the others.
